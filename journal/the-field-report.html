The Monday Field Report, Explained

Every Monday at eight, eleven phones buzz. By nine the week is decided. This is how the report works and why we publish it.

Published June 19, 2026 Read 6 min By Ada Roerig

The field report is not a procurement document, it is a conversation, and it happens by voice note more often than by text. Eleven farms inside thirty miles, plus the oyster bed across the river, each one sending what is ready, what is thin, and what surprised them over the weekend. A hail line on Saturday changes Monday's menu. A pig processed Friday changes the next three Mondays. The report is the menu's first draft, and the cooks write nothing down before it arrives.

Why publish it? Because a menu is a promise about a supply chain the guest never sees, and publishing the chain is the cheapest trust there is. When the letter goes out the first Monday of the month, it carries the report in plain language: what Grazin' Acres is butchering, what Fishkill's orchard is pressing, what the oyster bed's tides did. Guests plan dinners around truth instead of around a photograph. It costs us nothing and it has built more loyalty than any loyalty program ever invented.

There is a discipline to reading a report too. "Thin" means plan the backup now, not on Thursday. "Surprised" means taste it today, because surprises rot. "Ready" means the harvest is at its window, and windows close. The walk-in is a calendar of other people's weather, and the menu is what we do about it. A kitchen that waits for the order sheet is a kitchen that cooks from someone else's imagination.

The second use of the report is the one guests never think about: it prices the menu. When the report is rich, the kitchen runs specials that lower the effective cost of the tasting without touching the quality. When the report is lean, the menu is short and the portions are honest. Nobody dines here at a loss and nobody dines here at a markup that hides. The report keeps the whole ledger in daylight.
