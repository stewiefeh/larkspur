Reading a Menu Is Reading a Place

Menus are maps. Ours just happens to be drawn at the scale of thirty miles.

Published October 8, 2026 Read 6 min By Ada Roerig

The first thing a good menu tells you is not what to eat. It tells you how far the kitchen is willing to drive. Ours answers before you read a single dish: thirty miles, plus one river crossing for oysters, and that is the whole map. Once you see menus this way you cannot unsee it. A menu with octopus in Kansas City is not a menu, it is an airline schedule.

The order matters too. When a menu opens with raw things and closes with sweet ones, the kitchen is showing you its temperature range, the full run from cold water to hot sugar. When a menu opens with bread, it is telling you it has an opinion about time. Bread is the only dish that has to be finished before you sit down, so a kitchen that bakes in-house has already decided to work hours before service for a thing you eat in ninety seconds. That ratio, hours of work against seconds of eating, is the most honest number on any menu. Nothing else tells you as much about the people back there.

Count the verbs next. Menus written by committees describe nouns: a "seared duck breast with quince glaze." Menus written by cooks describe what the fire did: "breast over the hearth, carved at the pass." The second version is longer to write and shorter to read, which is why committees hate it. It is also the only version that can only be true in one kitchen. Any kitchen in the country can sear a duck. Only ours carves it standing next to the fire, because the fire and the pass are the same square meter.

Then look for the escape hatches. A menu full of "substitutions graciously accommodated" is a menu written by someone who does not trust their kitchen to adapt. Our menu has no exceptions section because adaptation is the default, not a printed policy. You tell your server what you cannot eat and the plate rebuilds around you. The menu is a starting sketch, the constraint is real, and the cooking happens after you arrive.

Read a menu the way you would read a letter from someone who lives somewhere. It is addressed to the valley first and to you second. The best ones know that order and do not apologize for it.
